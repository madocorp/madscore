Chapter 01 / The idea

Why Tristaff?

Tristaff puts pitch and time on one regular grid. You can start reading with two directions.

Rule: Up means a higher sound. Right means later in time. A note's height shows its pitch; its width shows how long it lasts.

Why make another way to write music?

Tristaff aims to make the first steps in reading music faster. Its rules repeat in the same way for every pitch, so there is less to remember at the start. It also aims to describe many kinds of music.

Pitch goes up

Pitch means how high or low a sound is.

Time goes right

A beat is a steady unit of time, like a clap.

Why is the page taller?

Each pitch gets its own height. That makes the pattern regular, but a Tristaff page may take more vertical space than a traditional music page.

A little history · If you want to go deeper

How music writing changed

People have written music in many ways. In medieval Europe, singers used small signs called neumes above words. Early neumes helped show the shape of a melody, but often did not give every pitch a fixed place.

Staff lines made pitch easier to locate. In the 11th century, Guido of Arezzo developed a system of lines to help singers learn unfamiliar melodies. Four-line staffs remained common in church chant. Western music later used staffs with different numbers of lines; the five-line staff became familiar for much written music.

A wider view

This is one part of music history. In Korea, jeongganbo uses a grid to organize music on a page. Chinese qin music books can include tunes and instructions for the player's fingers. These traditions show that music can be written for different needs, without using a five-line staff.

Different needs, different pages

The five-line staff holds a long musical history and a great deal of music. Tristaff offers another way to show pitch and time, with a regular semitone grid.
