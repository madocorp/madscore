Chapter 02 / Pitch

Find a pitch

One band holds twelve neighboring pitches, from A up to G♯.

Rule: The three lines hold A♯, D, and F♯. Move up or down one place for each neighboring piano key. The same pattern repeats around every line.

The teal notes below all belong to one melody: one line of music. Color shows which melody a note belongs to. Its height, not its color, shows its pitch.

Three lines to remember

The notes A♯, D, and F♯ sit on the three lines. Use each line as a starting point. The nearest pitch below is one step down; the nearest pitch above is one step up.

One step aboveBD♯G
On a lineA♯DF♯
One step belowAC♯F

The lines are four semitones apart. Between A♯ and D, the pitches are B, C, C♯. Between D and F♯, they are D♯, E, F. Above F♯ come G and G♯. The next step is A, which begins the next octave band; its A♯ sits on that band's lower line. The four-step spacing between lines repeats.

Music words

A pitch is how high or low a sound is. A semitone is one step between neighboring piano keys.

Sharp (♯) means one semitone higher than the letter alone. Flat (♭) means one semitone lower.

Think about it: Find C and D on the note map. C sits between the lower and middle lines; D sits on the middle line. The filled note between them is above a black piano key. Is it C♯ or D♭?

Show the answer

Both names are right. C♯ is one semitone above C, and D♭ is one semitone below D. They name the same black key and the same pitch.

Music science · If you want to go deeper

The chromatic scale

A scale is a set of pitches in order. The chromatic scale uses every neighboring pitch: all twelve semitone steps before the pattern starts again at the next A. The piano above shows one chromatic run from A to G♯; its final white A begins the next run.

In common equal temperament, each step changes frequency by the same ratio. Tristaff gives each step equal vertical space, so the pitch map stays regular.

How is a five-line staff different?

Tristaff: three lines

Each neighboring semitone gets its own height. A♯, D, and F♯ always mark the lines of a band. The map is regular, but it takes more vertical space.

Traditional staff: five lines

Lines and spaces follow note letters. A clef tells you which letters they mean. A sharp or flat changes a note's sound while it stays at the same staff height. For example, C and C♯ share a position.
